Case Study 01 · Service Innovation

Uber: One problem.
One question. A new way to move.

A human frustration became the starting point for a new on-demand transportation service. Innovation can come from connecting existing technologies in a new way.

🚘
From waiting for a ride to requesting one
"Fall in love with the problem, not with the technology."
01 · Set the scene

Take a journey to Paris

Imagine Paris in late 2008. Two technology entrepreneurs, Travis Kalanick and Garrett Camp, are visiting the city for the LeWeb technology conference.

They spend time discussing ideas and opportunities. The conversation connects with a frustration Garrett had experienced in San Francisco: finding a taxi when he needed one could be difficult.

Now picture the situation: it is late, you are tired, and you need to get back to your hotel. You look for a cab. You wait. You try again. Still no ride.

At that moment, even a donkey with a passenger seat might look like a luxury vehicle!

Most people stop at complaining. Innovators start questioning.

Historical note: The late-night Paris discussion is based on Travis Kalanick's account. The popular version that the founders were stranded outside a bar at 3 AM is a storytelling embellishment, not a verified detail.
02 · The turning-point question

What if getting a ride were as simple as using a phone?

What if you could request a car from your phone? What if the driver could find your location? What if you could see the car approaching? What if payment happened automatically?

Garrett's initial concept was an on-demand luxury car service, with the cost shared through an iPhone application. The idea developed into UberCab, later known as Uber.

The innovation spark: What if we could make requesting a ride simple, convenient and predictable?
03 · Existing technology, new combination

Did they invent all the technology?

No. The innovation was not the invention of every component. It was the way existing capabilities were brought together in a new service experience.

GPSLocation awareness
InternetConnecting people
SmartphonesUser interface
Digital mapsNavigation
PaymentsConvenient transactions
Driver networkDelivering the ride

Remember: You don't always have to reinvent the wheel. Sometimes you need to connect existing wheels in a different way.

04 · From idea to experiment

An idea had to meet reality

The team developed the early application and tested the service. An early test took place in New York in January 2010 using just three cars. Uber launched in San Francisco on May 31, 2010.

Three cars—not thousands. Early experiments let a team learn from real use, discover friction and improve before expanding.
05 · Design Thinking

Five stages, one human-centred mindset

Use the Uber journey to understand the five commonly taught stages of Design Thinking. This is a learning interpretation of the story, not a claim that the founders formally followed this exact framework.

01 · EMPATHIZE

🤝 Understand people

Recognise passenger frustration, uncertainty and the need for reliable transportation.

02 · DEFINE

🎯 Frame the problem

How might we help people request a reliable ride with less waiting and uncertainty?

03 · IDEATE

💡 Explore possibilities

Consider app-based requests, location sharing, driver matching and digital payments.

04 · PROTOTYPE

🧩 Make it tangible

Build an early app and service that people can try.

05 · TEST

🧪 Learn and improve

Try the service with users, gather feedback and refine it.

Design Thinking is iterative. Testing may reveal that the problem needs to be reframed or another idea explored.

06 · Think like an AIML engineer

Technology is a tool. Problem-solving is the skill.

Imagine you build a machine-learning model with 95% accuracy. That sounds impressive. But what if nobody needs the application? What if it is too slow, too expensive, difficult to use, or unreliable outside the lab?

Before choosing Python, machine learning, deep learning or generative AI, ask who experiences the problem and what evidence shows that it matters.

Ask yourself: Who is experiencing this problem? How do they solve it today? What makes the current approach frustrating? How will I know my solution helped?
07 · Your turn

Can you find the next big problem?

Look around your college, your home, your neighbourhood or your community. Find one genuine problem. Do not start by choosing a technology.

Five-minute innovation challenge

Start with a problem. Not a product.

Work in a team of 3–5. Discuss one problem that people experience and propose a way to improve their situation.

People face the problem of ________________________.
We propose to solve it by ________________________.
Using ________________________ technology.

Explain who has the problem, why it matters and how you would test your idea.

The takeaway

What should you remember?

Uber's story reminds us that innovation can come from recognising a human need, reframing a problem, combining existing capabilities and learning through experiments.

Don't just ask: What technology can I build?
Ask: What problem can I solve using technology?

Keep your eyes open. Keep questioning. Keep experimenting. The next meaningful idea may begin with a problem you notice on your way to college tomorrow.
